# B1 — The Glacier cost gotcha

The configured transition happens 30 days after upload. If the seller requests deletion on day
40, the object has spent only 10 days in S3 Glacier Flexible Retrieval. That storage class has a
90-day minimum storage duration. Deleting it early does not erase the minimum-duration charge:
AWS bills the remaining 80 days (prorated) of the 90-day minimum, in addition to any applicable
request/retrieval or other charges. The exact amount depends on the object's size, region, and
pricing; the important point is that immediate deletion can leave a charge for storage that is
no longer present.

**Redesign:** make the retention/deletion policy explicit before archiving. If the business
must honor deletion requests within 30 days, do not transition objects into a class with a
90-day minimum while they remain eligible for deletion. Keep them in S3 Standard (or another
appropriate class without that minimum) through the deletion-request eligibility window, then
transition only after the window has closed. For example, if the 30-day deletion window starts
at upload, transition no earlier than day 120 (30 days for request eligibility plus the
90-day minimum-storage risk window), subject to legal/product retention requirements. If
requests can arrive at any later point, a fixed delay alone cannot eliminate early-deletion
charges; use a policy decision that weighs expected early-delete costs against storage savings,
or choose a storage class whose minimum-duration and retrieval characteristics fit the
deletion SLA.

Operationally, model the request workflow and lifecycle together: mark the listing as pending
deletion, delete eligible objects within the promised deadline, and record lifecycle transitions
and deletion outcomes. Add cost monitoring/alerts for early-delete charges and test boundary
cases (request immediately before and after transition). Do not delay a legally required
deletion merely to avoid a storage charge; budget for the charge when compliance requires
immediate deletion.
